1 · What kind of compound is it?

Every naming problem starts with one decision. Look at the formula (or the name) and sort it into one of these groups; each group has its own rules.

  • Ionic: starts with a metal or the ammonium ion, NH4+. Name the cation, then the anion. No prefixes. Example: CaCl2, calcium chloride.
  • Molecular (binary): two nonmetals. Use prefixes (mono-, di-, tri-…) for the number of each atom. Example: N2O4, dinitrogen tetroxide.
  • Acid: H written first, dissolved in water (aq). Example: HCl(aq), hydrochloric acid; H2SO4, sulfuric acid.
  • Hydrate: an ionic compound with water in the crystal, written with a dot. Example: CuSO4·5H2O, copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate.

2 · Ionic compounds with fixed-charge metals

Group 1 metals are always +1, group 2 are always +2, and aluminum is always +3. Zinc (Zn2+), cadmium (Cd2+) and silver (Ag+) also have only one common charge.

  • Name the metal unchanged: sodium, magnesium, aluminum.
  • Name a one-atom anion by changing the ending of the element to -ide: chlorine → chloride, oxygen → oxide, nitrogen → nitride.
  • The charges fix the ratio, so no prefixes and no Roman numeral: MgCl2 is magnesium chloride, not magnesium dichloride.
Worked example
Al2O3: aluminum is a fixed-charge metal (Al3+), and O becomes oxide → aluminum oxide.
Mg3N2: Mg2+ and N3− → magnesium nitride. (Nitride, N3−, is different from nitrate, NO3−.)

3 · Variable-charge metals: Roman numerals

Most transition metals, plus tin and lead, form more than one ion. The name must give the charge as a Roman numeral in parentheses right after the metal: iron(II) is Fe2+, iron(III) is Fe3+.

To find the charge from a formula, work backward from the anion, whose charge you know:

Worked example · Fe2(SO4)3
1. Sulfate is SO42−. Three of them: 3 × (−2) = −6.
2. The compound is neutral, so the two iron ions together are +6.
3. Each iron is +6 ÷ 2 = +3 → iron(III) sulfate.
The mercury(I) trap
Mercury(I) exists as Hg22+, two Hg+ bonded together. So mercury(I) chloride is Hg2Cl2, not HgCl. Mercury(II) chloride is HgCl2.

Older names use -ous for the lower charge and -ic for the higher: ferrous (Fe2+), ferric (Fe3+), cuprous, cupric, stannous, stannic. You may see them on reagent bottles. They are accepted here, with a note giving the modern name.

4 · Polyatomic ions

A polyatomic ion is a group of atoms that carries a charge and stays together. Its name does not change in a compound: sodium sulfate, ammonium nitrate. These must be memorized; the full list is in the ion tables at the end.

The oxyanion pattern

For the same central element, the endings and prefixes track the number of oxygens. The charge stays the same.

NameFormulaRule
perchlorateClO4−per- … -ate: one more O than -ate
chlorateClO3−-ate: the reference ion
chloriteClO2−-ite: one fewer O than -ate
hypochloriteClO−hypo- … -ite: one fewer O than -ite

Bromine and iodine follow the same pattern (bromate BrO3−, periodate IO4−). For sulfur, nitrogen and phosphorus, learn the -ate ion and drop one oxygen for -ite: sulfate SO42− → sulfite SO32−.

Hydrogen-containing anions

Adding H+ to an anion makes its charge one less negative, and the name gains “hydrogen”: carbonate CO32− → hydrogen carbonate HCO3− (older name: bicarbonate); phosphate PO43− → hydrogen phosphate HPO42− → dihydrogen phosphate H2PO4−.

Parentheses

When a formula needs more than one of a polyatomic ion, put the ion in parentheses and write the count after it: Ca(NO3)2, (NH4)2SO4. Without parentheses, CaNO32 would mean 32 oxygens. With only one, no parentheses are needed: NaNO3.

5 · Writing a formula from a name

  1. Write each ion with its charge. The Roman numeral gives a variable metal's charge.
  2. Find the smallest numbers of each ion that make the total charge zero.
  3. Write the cation first, use subscripts for the counts, and add parentheses around a polyatomic ion that appears more than once.
Worked example · aluminum sulfate
Al3+ and SO42−. Least common multiple of 3 and 2 is 6.
2 Al3+ = +6; 3 SO42− = −6 → Al2(SO4)3.
Lowest ratio · lead(IV) oxide
Pb4+ and O2−: one Pb4+ balances two O2− → PbO2, not Pb2O4. Ionic formulas always use the lowest whole-number ratio.

6 · Binary molecular compounds

Two nonmetals share electrons instead of forming ions, so there are no charges to fix the ratio. The name states the count of each atom with a Greek prefix.

  • The first element gets a prefix only if there is more than one: CO2 is carbon dioxide, not monocarbon dioxide.
  • The second element always gets a prefix, even mono-: CO is carbon monoxide. Its ending changes to -ide.
  • Before “oxide”, the final a or o of the prefix is usually dropped: monoxide, tetroxide, pentoxide. The full spelling (tetraoxide) is also accepted.
  • The element further left or lower in the periodic table is usually written first; oxygen comes last except with fluorine (OF2, oxygen difluoride).
Worked examples
N2O5 → dinitrogen pentoxide. PCl5 → phosphorus pentachloride. P4O10 → tetraphosphorus decoxide.

7 · Acids

An acid here is a compound of H+ with an anion, dissolved in water. There are as many H as needed to balance the anion's charge: SO42− needs two, so H2SO4.

Anion ends inAcid nameExample
-ide (no oxygen)hydro- … -ic acidCl− chloride → HCl(aq) hydrochloric acid
-ate… -ic acidNO3− nitrate → HNO3 nitric acid
-ite… -ous acidNO2− nitrite → HNO2 nitrous acid

Prefixes stay: perchlorate → perchloric acid, hypochlorite → hypochlorous acid. A few roots change slightly: sulfate → sulfuric, sulfite → sulfurous, phosphate → phosphoric. Hydro- is only for acids without oxygen, so “hydrosulfuric acid” is H2S, not H2SO4.

8 · Hydrates

Name the ionic compound, then add a prefix for the number of waters plus “hydrate”. The formula shows the waters after a dot.

Worked example
CuSO4·5H2O → copper(II) sulfate + penta (5) + hydrate → copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate.
MgSO4·7H2O → magnesium sulfate heptahydrate (Epsom salt).

When typing, use a period or an asterisk for the dot: CuSO4.5H2O or CuSO4*5H2O.

9 · How your answers are checked

The rule is simple: if your answer pins down exactly one compound, it passes. The tool turns your name or formula into a structure and compares it with the answer, so wording details don't cost you.

  • Capitals and spaces in names don't matter: “Iron (III) chloride” passes.
  • Answers that are correct but not standard pass with a note showing the standard form: an unneeded Roman numeral (zinc(II) chloride), prefixes on an ionic compound (iron trichloride), an older name (ferric chloride), British spelling (sulphate).
  • Answers that could mean more than one compound do not pass: “iron chloride” (FeCl2 or FeCl3?) or “carbon oxide” (CO or CO2?).
  • Formulas are case-sensitive, because they have to be: Co is cobalt, CO is carbon monoxide. Ionic formulas must use the lowest ratio, and a polyatomic ion used more than once needs parentheses.
  • For an ion on its own, include the charge: SO4^2-, SO4 2- or SO4(2-).

Drawing structures (+5 bonus)

When you are asked for the formula of a molecule, an acid or a polyatomic ion, you can draw its Lewis structure instead of typing the formula. A correct drawing earns 15 points instead of 10 in a quiz.

  • Draw every atom, including every hydrogen, and every lone pair. Mark every nonzero formal charge.
  • For atoms in period 3 and below (S, P, Cl…), use the structure that minimizes formal charges, which may expand the octet: sulfate is drawn with two S=O bonds.
  • Any one valid resonance form counts.
  • If your drawing shows the right compound but has a Lewis-structure mistake (a missing lone pair, an unmarked charge), it still earns the base 10 points, and the feedback shows what to fix.
  • Ionic compounds can't be drawn as single molecules, so for those you type the formula.
